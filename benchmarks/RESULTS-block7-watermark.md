Partial-alpha pixels exist, so the two hypotheses are testable. Over **439,105**
partial pixels across 9 frames spanning the clip (0, 8, 15, 23, 30, 38, 45, 53, 60):

| hypothesis | prediction | measured |
|---|---|---|
| premultiplied against black | no channel exceeds its alpha | 0.0000% of pixels violate it |
| straight | colour is unscaled and routinely exceeds alpha | 100.0000% of pixels violate it |

Largest excess over alpha: **0** levels of 255; mean excess where it
occurs: **0.00** levels. Tolerance 2 levels, absorbing the
12-bit-to-8-bit and YUV-to-RGB round trip.

**Do the two hypotheses actually separate here?** They only do on bright artwork:
dark colour never exceeds its alpha under either reading, so a premultiplied verdict
on a dark logo would say more about the logo than the file.

| | |
|---|---|
| fully opaque pixels | 16,774,277 |
| mean max(r,g,b) there | **252.7** of 255 (separation needs > 128) |
| mean max(r,g,b)/alpha on partial pixels | **0.9854** (premultiplied tends to 1) |

**Verdict: premultiplied (against black).** Colour is already scaled down by alpha
across effectively every partial pixel. Importing it as straight would divide out an
alpha that was never multiplied in and brighten the edge.

The artwork is essentially white at 252.7, so under a straight
reading a half-transparent edge pixel would still carry near-255 colour. It carries
0.9854 of its alpha instead — the colour has been multiplied in.
